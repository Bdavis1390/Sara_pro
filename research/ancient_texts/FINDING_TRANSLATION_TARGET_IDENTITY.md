# Finding: Translation Target Identity Is a First-Class Problem

## The deeper question

Translation auditing normally asks:

> Does the target-language sentence correctly represent the source-language sentence?

For complex ancient traditions, that is sometimes too late.

The first question is:

> **Which source text-object are we translating?**

## 1 Enoch as a control case

Scholarly work on 1 Enoch shows why.

The complete Ethiopic collection survives in a later manuscript tradition and derives through a Greek intermediary from earlier Semitic material.

The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve multiple Aramaic Enochic works and fragments.

Recent scholarship emphasizes that the Ethiopic book should not simply be collapsed into the several Aramaic work-forms. The problem is not merely missing words; it can involve:
- collection boundaries;
- ordering;
- expansion/contraction;
- recension;
- composition history;
- different literary objects.

The astronomical material is particularly clear. Nickelsburg and VanderKam distinguish the Aramaic **Astronomical Book** from the Ethiopic **Book of the Luminaries**, stating that the latter is indirectly based on the former through Greek but differs significantly from it.

## Translation consequence

A translation may target:

### Witness translation
A specific manuscript or inscription.

### Recension translation
A historically identifiable textual form represented by multiple related witnesses.

### Daughter-version translation
A later translation tradition, such as Geʿez, treated as a text in its own right.

### Reconstructed critical translation
A scholarly reconstruction assembled from multiple witnesses/languages.

### Canonical-collection translation
A later collection as transmitted in a religious or literary tradition.

These products can all be legitimate.

They must not be silently presented as the same thing.

## New failure mode

A translation can be internally accurate but externally misleading if its label hides textual synthesis.

For example:

"Translation of 1 Enoch"

is under-specified if the actual product is:

"Critical English reconstruction using Ethiopic, Greek and Aramaic witnesses."

That distinction affects:
- historical claims;
- chronology;
- authorship/composition analysis;
- quotation;
- theological interpretation;
- linguistic reconstruction.

## ATIP rule

Every consequential translation must declare its target identity before receiving a claim state.

This extends the Translation Lineage Graph upward:

work family
-> text-object identity
-> witness/recension
-> transcription
-> translation.

The "work" itself is now versioned/provenanced evidence.
